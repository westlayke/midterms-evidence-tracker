Summary and top 10 · Full evidence & methods

Summary

Short version, as of Nov 22, 2026. Republicans lost a referendum on President Trump, driven by prices, the economy and tariffs. About 60% of the swing came from who voted (Trump 2024 voters staying home, Democrats and new voters turning out) and about 40% from Trump voters switching sides. Across methods, the turnout share runs from 50% to 75%.
  • 1. It was a vote against Trump. Voters said they were voting against Trump: 43% to 16% in the SSRS Voter Poll (NBC) and 48% to 15% in Pew's post-election survey (Pew), a wider gap than after the 2018 midterm (39% to 25%).Why it matters: this is the motive behind the loss. The election was mainly a verdict on Trump, not on Democrats or on local races, and the anti-Trump vote was bigger than the anti-Biden vote after 2022 (35% to 17%).How sure we are: high. Two large surveys with different designs give the same answer. Open: people report their own motives, and these figures do not show whether the motive worked by changing votes or by keeping voters home.
  • 2. Prices, the economy and tariffs drove it. 75% rated the economy poorly, 65% said tariffs hurt it and 62% blamed higher prices mostly on Trump's policies (SSRS via NBC).Why it matters: it explains why voters turned on Trump. Other polls agree: J.L. Partners found cost of living and the economy were the top two reasons in every group, On Point found 77.8% of Trump voters who switched to Democrats said gas prices pushed them, and Navigator's post-election poll (Democratic-aligned, opt-in) found 60% put inflation and the cost of living among their top five issues (Navigator).Still open: 63% called the Iran war the wrong decision, and the war pushed fuel prices up at the same time, so surveys cannot cleanly separate the war from prices.
  • 3. Republicans staying home did more damage than switching. NORC's 113,020-voter survey, as broken down by Patrick Ruffini, puts about 60% of the swing on turnout and 40% on switching (Ruffini); Lenny Bronner's precinct model puts turnout at 75% (Bronner).Why it matters: this is the key question for 2028. If most of the loss came from Republicans staying home, the party can recover by bringing them back; if it came from switching, the problem is persuasion.How sure we are: fairly sure turnout was the larger part, because a large survey, precinct returns and the Georgia voter file land in the same range. The exact split is not settled: estimates run from 50% (Cygnal) to 75%, NORC identifies who stayed home with a model rather than turnout records, and state voter files (expected around the week of Nov 30) should pin it down.
  • 4. Switching was real but modest. About 7% of Trump 2024 voters who voted backed a House Democrat, and about 1 in 10 in Texas, Ohio, Iowa, Kansas and South Carolina (CNN).Why it matters: switching was not the main story, but about 7 in 100 Trump voters crossing over is enough to flip close races, and it was larger in the red states where Democrats won Senate seats.How sure we are: fairly sure of the 7%, because SSRS and NORC, two separate surveys, both find it. Other polls find more (J.L. Partners about 12% of those who voted, On Point 15.1%), partly because they count on different bases, so the true size is still debated.
  • 5. Latino and young voters swung back. Majority-Hispanic districts moved about 22 points toward Democrats, against about 14 in districts under 10% Hispanic (Morris); youth turnout of 27% was the second highest for a midterm in 30 years (CIRCLE).Why it matters: these groups had moved toward Trump in 2024, so their return undid much of his coalition gain. SSRS (via NBC) has Latino voters at 62% for House Democrats after 46% for Trump in 2024, and men under 25 at 68–28 Democratic after 49–48 for Trump.How sure we are: high on direction, because election returns back the surveys. Less on size: the 2024 figures come from different surveys, so each swing mixes methods.
  • 6. Texas is the exception. There, voters switching to Democrats, not Republicans staying home, explains most of the Democratic gain, which fits voters punishing the ticket for Ken Paxton (Bronner, Profoundd).Why it matters: it shows candidates still mattered in places. Only 34% of Texas voters rated Paxton's character positively (SSRS).What it does not show: weak candidates did not drive the national result. Statewide candidates finished only 1.3 points from their party's House vote on average (Adam Bonica, cited by Ruffini), so candidates explain a few states, not the wave.
  • 7. Redistricting limited the losses rather than causing them. Against the old maps, Republicans still gained about 4–7 seats (NYT via PollTracker).Why it matters: this rules out a popular explanation, that the mid-decade map changes backfired. Headlines saying so count outcomes (CNN: Republicans won only 2 of the 15 districts they redrew), while the New York Times compared the result with the old maps, which answers the question of cause. North Carolina was the only slight backfire.How sure we are: fairly sure of the direction; the 4–7 seat range is a model estimate.
  • 8. The Trump voters who stayed home look recoverable. They still lean Republican for the House (73% to 10%) but are more moderate and sourer on Trump (Robinson, NORC data).Why it matters: most of the Trump voters who skipped 2026 would still have voted Republican, so the loss does not look like a permanent shift. But compared with Trump voters who turned out they are more moderate (44% vs. 27%), gloomier on the economy (61% vs. 39% say it is poor) and more likely to disapprove of Trump (45% vs. 20%), so they are not safe Republican votes.Still open: who stayed home comes from NORC's model, not turnout records, and Morris's November poll finds many of them now prefer Democrats.

Where the evidence converges

This tracker's ranking and independent reviews by Claude and ChatGPT put the same four causes on top, in the same order: the referendum on Trump over prices, the turnout gap, switching by Trump 2024 voters, and Latino and young voters swinging back. All three put turnout at about 60% of the swing.

The top-scored sources agree on the motive: Pew and SSRS, two different survey designs, both find a vote against Trump. They agree less on the mechanism. Estimates of the turnout share run from 50% (Cygnal) to 75% (Bronner), and Split Ticket and On Point find more switching. Validated voter files should settle the split.

District returns point the same way as the motive surveys: against the 2024 presidential result, about 99% of House districts moved toward Democrats, a more uniform shift than in any midterm back to 1994 (Jordan), and the spread of candidates' over- and under-performance is the narrowest since at least 2014 (Jain). Both are early compilations without linked data.

Does engagement track quality?

No. Among the 12 analyst posts scored blind on a 0–12 scale, more views went with lower quality (Spearman −0.68, p = 0.015). In the widened sample of 62 posts the link disappears (+0.22, p = 0.08). On the agreed 1–100 rubric, the 25 scored X posts show no link with views (−0.16, p = 0.45), and higher-scored posts drew fewer likes, reposts, replies, quotes and bookmarks per view (−0.51, p = 0.01).

Caveats: the samples are small, the posts differ in age, 7 of the 25 rubric-scored posts are Seth Keshel's, and p-values are not corrected for running many tests. How the finders work (Method and limits)

How the scores work

Each item gets 0–100 on seven criteria. Its score is the weighted average, rounded, from 1 to 100. A high score means the analysis passed every test, not that its explanation is proven.

1. Factual accuracy
Do the facts, numbers and quotes match the original source when checked?		20%
2. Evidence quality
Is it built on real data that fits the claim, with the method disclosed (for polls: who was asked, sample size, weighting, dates, margin of error)?		20%
3. Causal reasoning
Does it show the factor actually moved the vote, tested against the strongest rival explanation, and separate staying home from switching?		25%
4. Corroboration
Does independent evidence (official returns, voter files, other surveys) back it up?		10%
5. Calibration
Are the conclusions as strong as the evidence, and no stronger, with uncertainty stated?		10%
6. Track record
How has the source done on similar claims since 2018? A partisan lean counts only if its misses went the same way by more than 3 points in 2 of its last 3 cycles.		10%
7. Independence
Are party, client or funding ties disclosed, and did they bend the evidence?		5%

Caps: if factual accuracy scores 25 or less, or fabrication is found, the score cannot go above 40. If causal reasoning scores 25 or less, it cannot go above 55.

Not applicable: only corroboration (when independent data is not published yet) and track record (when a search finds no comparable past record) can be set aside, at most 20% of the weight. The rest is reweighted and the score gets an asterisk as provisional. Factual accuracy and causal reasoning can never be set aside.

Reading a score: 85–100 highly reliable, 70–84 generally reliable, 50–69 mixed, 30–49 weak, 1–29 unreliable.

How the criteria were agreed

  1. Separate first drafts. Grok Bot wrote its own proposal first. ChatGPT (gpt-6.2-astra) and Claude (claude-fable-5-2) then got the same neutral brief and each proposed criteria and weights without seeing Grok Bot's or each other's.
  2. Anonymous cross-review. Each reviewed the other two proposals with the authors hidden. Claude added corroboration and proposed the caps; ChatGPT adopted the caps. Grok Bot, a participant as well as the moderator, gave ground: causal reasoning up to 25%, track record down to 10% and limited to comparable claims.
  3. Shared drafts. Grok Bot merged the views into drafts and ran the rounds. In round 3 all three accepted every weight (20/20/25/10/10/10/5); in round 4 Claude accepted the draft as is.
  4. Last point settled. After four rounds ChatGPT still wanted tighter track-record and n/a wording. The tracker's owner adopted ChatGPT's wording, with the 3-point threshold that Grok Bot had fixed before researching any track records. Claude accepted it, noting that a flat 3-point test can also flag years when nearly all polls missed, such as 2020, so it is read alongside the industry average.
  5. Tested. Applied to 18 items whose quality was known in advance (7 good, 5 mixed, 6 bad), the rubric ranked all 42 good-versus-bad pairs correctly, with a rank correlation of 0.92.
