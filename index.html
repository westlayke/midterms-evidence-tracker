How the scores work ↓

Why Republicans lost: eight findings

  • 1. It was a vote against Trump. Voters said they were voting against Trump: 43% to 16% in the SSRS Voter Poll (NBC) and 48% to 15% in Pew's post-election survey (Pew), a wider gap than after the 2018 midterm (39% to 25%).Why it matters: this is the motive behind the loss. The election was mainly a verdict on Trump, not on Democrats or on local races, and the anti-Trump vote was bigger than the anti-Biden vote after 2022 (35% to 17%).How sure we are: high. Two large surveys with different designs give the same answer, though SSRS did the fieldwork for both (Pew methods), so they are not fully independent. Open: people report their own motives, and these figures do not show whether the motive worked by changing votes or by keeping voters home.How we got this: each figure is the share of voters who said their House vote was to oppose Trump, or to support him; the rest said he was not a factor. SSRS polled more than 79,000 voters (NBC write-up) and Pew 7,692 voters, Nov 9–15. The 2018 (39–25) and 2022 (35–17, about Biden) figures are the same Pew question in its earlier post-election surveys (Pew topline).
  • 2. Prices, the economy and tariffs drove it. In the SSRS Voter Poll of more than 79,000 voters, 75% rated the economy poorly, 65% said tariffs hurt it and 62% blamed higher prices mostly on Trump's policies (SSRS via NBC).Why it matters: it explains why voters turned on Trump. Other polls agree: J.L. Partners found cost of living and the economy were the top two reasons in every group, On Point found 77.8% of Trump voters who switched to Democrats said gas prices pushed them, from about 65 respondents, which On Point calls directional (On Point), and Navigator's post-election poll (Democratic-aligned, opt-in) found 60% put inflation and the cost of living among their top five issues (Navigator).Still open: in the same poll 63% called the Iran war the wrong decision, and the war pushed fuel prices up at the same time, so surveys cannot cleanly separate the war from prices.How we got this: every figure is a published share of respondents. The SSRS figures (75%, 65%, 62%, 63%) are of all voters. On Point's 77.8% is of the Trump-to-Democrat switchers in its poll. Navigator's 60% is of all its 3,000 registered voters, who ranked up to five issues (23% put inflation first). In J.L. Partners' poll, 33% of 2026 voters named cost of living or the economy as their reason and no other issue topped 9% (J.L. Partners).
  • 3. Who voted mattered more than switching, but by how much is unsettled. Turnout, including new voters, accounts for about 60% of the swing in NORC's 113,020-person survey as broken down by Patrick Ruffini (Ruffini) and in Lenny Bronner's Georgia check (Bronner, Georgia). It is about 75% in Bronner's 11-state precinct model (Bronner). J.L. Partners' Election Day poll, which scores 49 and so is left out of the chart, puts it at 67% (J.L. Partners). The New York Times' Voter Trends estimates do not settle the split. People who voted in 2026 but did not vote in their state in 2024, a group that includes the newly eligible and people who moved, backed House Democrats by 30 points; 2024 voters backed them by 11 (NYT Voter Trends). Against Trump's 1.5-point win in 2024, most of the swing, about 12.5 points, came among 2024 voters, where these figures cannot tell turnout from switching; new voters add about 3 points. So they give weak support to the turnout side at most. Among 2024 voters alone, Ruffini puts the turnout effect and switching at about the same size (5–6 points of margin each), with new voters adding 3.Why it matters: this is the key question for 2028. If most of the loss came from Republicans staying home, the party can recover by bringing them back; if it came from switching, the problem is persuasion.How sure we are: fairly sure turnout was the larger part, because a large survey, precinct returns and Georgia's records of who voted all point that way. The exact split is not settled. The sources differ mainly on how big the turnout gap was, NORC identifies who stayed home with a model rather than turnout records, and state voter files (expected around the week of Nov 30) should pin it down.How we got this: turnout share = turnout points ÷ total swing. Ruffini: (5.5 + 3) ÷ 14 ≈ 60%. J.L. Partners: 10 of a 15-point swing (R+2 to D+13) is 67%. Bronner publishes shares directly: 75% turnout and 20% switching in 11 states (5% unassigned), about 40% switching in Georgia. Bronner also says the exit poll implies 35% switching, but our arithmetic from the exit poll's published crossover rates gives 13–22% (see the summary note), so that reading is not used as an estimate here. NYT: published modeled margins from Times Voter Trends (Times/Siena interviews applied to the L2 voter file and calibrated to precinct results); 2024 voters went D+11 against R+1.5 in 2024, so 11 + 1.5 = 12.5 points of the swing came among them, a mix of differential turnout and switching. The Times does not give the new group's size; if it is about 15% of 2026 voters (NORC via Ruffini puts returning 2024 voters at about 85%), 0.85 × 11 + 0.15 × 30 ≈ 13.9, matching the D+13.9 count, so new voters add about 13.9 − 11 ≈ 3 points. Its group is people who did not vote in their state in 2024, not only people who skipped (added Nov 23; corrected Nov 23 after Kip's run-2 fact-check).
  • 4. Switching was real but modest. About 7% of Trump 2024 voters who voted backed a House Democrat (CNN), and about 1 in 10 backed the Democratic Senate candidates who won in Texas, Ohio, Iowa, Kansas and South Carolina (same CNN report). NORC finds the same 7% (Robinson, NORC data). NBC's write-up of the poll gives 6%, and 3% of Harris voters went the other way (NBC). These are shares of Trump voters, not of the swing; see the note above for how they relate.Why it matters: switching was not the main story, but about 7 in 100 Trump voters crossing over is enough to flip close races, and it was larger in the red states where Democrats won Senate seats.How sure we are: fairly sure of the 7%, because SSRS and NORC, two separate surveys, both find it. Other polls find more (J.L. Partners about 12% of those who voted, On Point 15.1%), partly because they count on different bases, so the true size is still debated.How we got this: 7% is CNN's share of self-reported Trump 2024 voters who voted in 2026 and backed the House Democrat; 'about 1 in 10' is CNN's figure for those five Senate races, not House races. NORC's 7% is the share of Trump voters who voted and backed the House Democrat in Robinson's chart. J.L. Partners says 'just under one in ten Trump voters (8%) switched' without stating the base. If the base is all 2024 Trump voters, 35% of whom did not vote, that is 8 ÷ 0.65 ≈ 12% of those who voted; this is our assumption, not J.L.'s figure (J.L. Partners). On Point's 15.1% counts registered Trump voters who voted for, or would have voted for, a Democrat, so it includes nonvoters' stated preferences (On Point).
  • 5. Latino and young voters swung back. In majority-Hispanic House districts the Democratic margin rose about 22 points from Harris's 2024 result, against about 14 in districts under 10% Hispanic (Morris; district returns, 2026 House vs. 2024 president). Youth turnout was about 27%, CIRCLE's early estimate (range 25–29%). That matches its early estimate for 2022 and trails only 2018 in 30 years, so young voters swung mainly in how they voted, not in how many turned out (CIRCLE).Why it matters: these groups had moved toward Trump in 2024, so their return undid much of his coalition gain. SSRS (via NBC, Nov 4) has Latino voters at 62% for House Democrats after 46% for Trump in 2024, and men under 25 at 68–28 Democratic after 49–48 for Trump (NBC). CNN's later tables of the same poll have Latinos at 65–33 (per Morris).How sure we are: high on direction, because election returns back the surveys. Less on size: the 2024 figures come from different surveys, so each swing mixes methods.How we got this: Morris's 22 and 14 points are the change in the Democratic margin from Harris's 2024 result to the 2026 House vote, for districts grouped by Hispanic share. 27% is CIRCLE's exit-poll-based estimate of turnout among young voters, with a 25–29% range. The 62% and 68–28 are SSRS figures for 2026 House votes; the 46% and 49–48 are the 2024 figures as NBC reports them.
  • 6. Texas is the exception. There, Bronner's model finds voters switching to Democrats explained more of the Democratic gain than turnout did, the only state where it did (Bronner, Texas, Profoundd). That fits voters punishing the ticket for Ken Paxton. Ray Tye's county analysis finds both at work: about 27% of 2024 Trump voters stayed home against about 14% of Harris voters, while 2–8% of Trump voters backed Talarico (Ray Tye).Why it matters: it shows candidates still mattered in places. Only 34% of Texas voters rated Paxton's character positively (SSRS Texas sample; NBC).What it does not show: weak candidates did not drive the national result. House candidates' over- and under-performance had its narrowest spread since at least 2014: one standard deviation of Split Ticket's WAR scores was 3.0 points in 2026, against 3.9 in 2024 and 8.6 in 2014 (Jain). So candidates explain a few states, not the wave.How we got this: Bronner's Texas result is his precinct model run on 12 counties holding 63% of the vote (Bronner, Texas). 34% is the share of SSRS's Texas voters who rated Paxton's character positively. Tye's 27% and 14% are his two-method estimates of 2024 voters who skipped 2026, and 2–8% his range for Trump voters who backed Talarico. Jain's chart plots each House race's WAR score (the result against a generic-candidate baseline) with 1, 2 and 3 standard-deviation bands; 3.0, 3.9 and 8.6 are its 1-SD lines, read off the chart. The method is in The Argument (paywalled past the method); no linked data.
  • 7. Redistricting limited the losses rather than causing them. Against the old maps, Republicans still gained about 4–7 seats, the New York Times' preliminary estimate from comparing the results with the maps they replaced (NYT, via PollTracker).Why it matters: this rules out a popular explanation, that the mid-decade map changes backfired. Headlines saying so count outcomes (CNN: Republicans flipped just 2 of the 15 districts they targeted, with a Louisiana runoff pending; CNN), while the New York Times compared the result with the old maps, which answers the question of cause. North Carolina is the one exception. The New York Times: "There is one state where the Republican effort seems to have backfired, if only barely: North Carolina" (its table: 0 to 1 seat to Democrats). Cain and Schneer say it will count as a backfire if the Democrat wins NC-3, which was still being counted (Cain and Schneer).How sure we are: fairly sure of the direction; the 4–7 seat range is a model estimate.How we got this: the New York Times estimated how many seats the 2026 vote would have given each party on the maps in place before 2025 and compared that with the actual result; 4–7 is its preliminary range, and its North Carolina row is +0 to +1 Democratic. CNN's 2 of 15 counts the targeted districts Republicans flipped, using CNN's race calls as of its Nov 17 update.
  • 8. The Trump voters who stayed home look recoverable. They still lean Republican for the House (73% to 10%) but are more moderate and sourer on Trump (Robinson, NORC data).Why it matters: most of the Trump voters who skipped 2026 would still have voted Republican, so the loss does not look like a permanent shift. But compared with Trump voters who turned out they are more moderate (44% vs. 27%), gloomier on the economy (61% vs. 39% say it is poor) and more likely to disapprove of Trump (45% vs. 20%), so they are not safe Republican votes.Still open: who stayed home comes from NORC's model, not turnout records, and Morris reports that his November poll finds many of them now prefer Democrats (Morris; behind a paywall, not checked here).How we got this: every figure is from Robinson's chart of NORC's 2024 Trump voters (Nov. 6 weights), comparing 1,760 whom NORC's likely-voter model counts as staying home with 34,353 who voted. Each pair is the share of stay-homes vs. voters giving that answer. The House preference leaves out the 19% of stay-homes who gave none.

Article quality relationship to reader engagement

On our data, how widely a post travels says little about its quality. Among the 26 X posts scored on the agreed 1–100 rubric, views show no link with score (Spearman −0.07, p = 0.74), but higher-scored posts drew fewer likes, reposts, replies, quotes and bookmarks per view (−0.48, p = 0.01). In the full sample of 116 posts scored on the blind 0–12 scale, views again show none (−0.10, p = 0.26) and the interaction pattern is stronger (−0.57, p less than 0.001); among the 17 analyst posts, a weak negative link with views (−0.29, p = 0.25) is not significant, down from the first result (−0.68, p = 0.015, 12 analyst posts scored blind, Nov 21 metrics) as posts were added. Weak posts are not seen more on average, but they draw more reaction from those who see them, and several of the most-viewed posts we recorded have documented errors (below).Caveats: small samples; posts of different ages; 7 of the 26 rubric-scored posts and 49 of the 116 are Seth Keshel's, added at the user's request and scored after their metrics were seen; one coder; no correction for many tests. Metrics fetched Nov 21, 2026, 9:00 AM ET; the two posts added Nov 22 were fetched Nov 22, 9:00 AM ET, under a day old, so their views will grow. John Couvillon's Louisiana thread (added and fetched Nov 23, 9:01 AM ET, two days old) is in the rubric figures only, as it was not blind-scored.How we got this: Spearman rank correlations, computed here, between views and quality score, with two-sided p-values (rubric p-values from 20,000 random permutations). Interactions per view = (likes + reposts + replies + quotes + bookmarks) ÷ views. Analyst and full-sample figures use the blind 0–12 score, rubric figures the 1–100 score. Bronner's Nov 5 model post (93,178 views, fetched Nov 22) moved the rubric views figure from −0.16 to −0.06; adding Couvillon's thread (51*, 17,542 views) on Nov 23 moved the rubric figures to −0.07 (p = 0.74) and −0.48 (p = 0.01), from −0.06 and −0.51. Short version

As of Nov 22, 2026 (turnout vs. switching corrected Nov 22). Republicans lost a referendum on President Trump, driven by prices, the economy and tariffs. Most of the roughly 15.5-point swing came from who voted, not from switching, but the split is not settled. In sources at or above the top-15 cutoff (68), turnout including new voters is about 60–75% of the swing: about 60% in NORC's survey as broken down by Patrick Ruffini (68; Ruffini) and in Bronner's Georgia check (68*; Bronner, Georgia), 75% in Lenny Bronner's 11-state precinct model (72*; Bronner). Switching is about 20–40% (Georgia check about 40%). Lower-scoring estimates: J.L. Partners' Election Day poll (49) 67% turnout (J.L. Partners), Cygnal's poll (38) about half (Cygnal), and the exit poll's crossover rates about 13–22% switching on our arithmetic (below), which rests partly on J.L. Partners.How we got this: the swing runs from Trump +1.5 in 2024 (49.8% to 48.3%; FEC) to D+13.9 in the unofficial, still-updating 2026 House count of 118.4 million votes (Popular Vote Analytics; Wikipedia gives D+14.1), about 15.5 points; earlier counts were lower (D+12.6 on Nov 6, per On Point). Turnout share = turnout points ÷ total. Ruffini: (5.5 + 3 new voters) ÷ 14 ≈ 60% (57–64% across his 5–6 point ranges). J.L. Partners: 10 of 15 = 67% (49, below the cutoff, not charted). Bronner publishes 75% turnout and 20% switching, 5% unassigned. J.L. Partners' 67% and 33% sit inside the 60–75% and 20–40% ranges, so leaving it out changes neither."7% of Trump voters switched" is a share of voters, not of the swing. Our rough arithmetic: 2024 voters were about 85% of 2026 voters (NORC via Ruffini); Trump voters were 46–50% of them (46% if 35% of Trump and 22% of Harris voters stayed home, per J.L. Partners; 50% at equal rates). So 6–7% of Trump voters is 2.3–3% of all voters, 4.7–6 points of margin (each switch moves the margin two votes). Netting out the 3% of Harris voters who voted Republican (NBC) leaves about 2–3.4 points, 13–22% of a 15.5-point swing. Ruffini finds more switching (5–6 points) partly because he corrects recalled 2024 votes for recall bias; his "switching" is net across all 2024 voters, and his "turnout" includes Harris voters turning out more. Not charted: its low end uses J.L. Partners' stay-home rates (49).Still open: all are surveys or models, and NORC's turnout is modelled, not checked against records. State voter files (expected around the week of Nov 30) should settle the split.

How we created the chart

Swing = 2024 Trump margin + 2026 Democratic House margin. 2024, official result, FEC (Federal Election Commission): Trump 49.8%, Harris 48.3%, so 49.8 − 48.3 = R+1.5 (FEC). 2026, unofficial: D+13.9 of 118.4 million votes counted, Popular Vote Analytics' counts as of Nov 15, 11:42 PM ET, unchanged when last checked Nov 23, 10:48 AM ET (Popular Vote Analytics); and D+14.1, Wikipedia's 56.3% to 42.2% (56.3 − 42.2 = 14.1), which cites the Cook Political Report House Vote Tracker as of Nov 17 (Wikipedia; Cook tracker, free to open, but its figures did not load as text for us). So 1.5 + 13.9 = 15.4 and 1.5 + 14.1 = 15.6. Unofficial = running count; states have not finished certifying. Where the evidence converges

The tracker's ranking and Claude's and ChatGPT's reviews of it (Nov 21; AI readings of the same evidence, not independent data) put the same four causes on top, in order: the referendum on Trump over prices, the turnout gap, switching by Trump 2024 voters, and Latino and young voters swinging back. Both put turnout at about 60% (ranges 45–75%, Claude; 50–75%, ChatGPT, not ruling out below 50%).

Pew and SSRS, two survey designs, agree on the motive: a vote against Trump. On the mechanism, the main sources run from about 60% turnout (NORC via Ruffini; Bronner's Georgia check) to about 75% (Bronner's 11 states); Cygnal (not on the leaderboard) puts it near 50%, and Split Ticket and On Point find more switching, so the full range on this page is about 50–75%. Validated voter files should settle it.

District returns agree: about 99% of House districts moved toward Democrats from the 2024 presidential result, more uniform than any midterm back to 1994 (Jordan), and the spread of candidates' over- and under-performance is the narrowest since at least 2014 (Jain); both are early compilations without linked data. How we got this: Jordan compares each district's 2026 House margin with its 2024 presidential margin; Jain measures how widely candidates ran ahead of or behind their party's expected vote. How the finders work (Method and limits)

How the scores work

Each item gets 0–100 on seven criteria. Its score is the weighted average, rounded, from 1 to 100. A high score means the analysis passed every test, not that its explanation is proven.

1. Factual accuracy
Do the facts, numbers and quotes match the original source when checked?		20%
2. Evidence quality
Is it built on real data that fits the claim, with the method disclosed (for polls: who was asked, sample size, weighting, dates, margin of error)?		20%
3. Causal reasoning
Does it show the factor actually moved the vote, tested against the strongest rival explanation, and separate staying home from switching?		25%
4. Corroboration
Does independent evidence (official returns, voter files, other surveys) back it up?		10%
5. Calibration
Are the conclusions as strong as the evidence, and no stronger, with uncertainty stated?		10%
6. Track record
How has the source done on similar claims since 2018? A partisan lean counts only if its misses went the same way by more than 3 points in 2 of its last 3 cycles.		10%
7. Independence
Are party, client or funding ties disclosed, and did they bend the evidence?		5%

Caps: factual accuracy of 25 or less, or fabrication, caps the score at 40; causal reasoning of 25 or less caps it at 55. Only corroboration (independent data not published yet) and track record (no comparable past record) can be set aside, at most 20% of the weight; the rest is reweighted and the score gets an asterisk as provisional. Factual accuracy and causal reasoning never can.

Reading a score: 85–100 highly reliable, 70–84 generally reliable, 50–69 mixed, 30–49 weak, 1–29 unreliable. The leaderboard shows the top 15 (10 before Nov 23, 2026); the cutoff is the #15 score, now 68; ties go by name; Days count days on the top 15.

Chart rule (owner, Nov 23, 2026): only sources at or above the top-15 cutoff (now 68) set a chart's main bars or ranges. Sources scoring 55 to 67 appear only in a lighter shade, labelled with their score, and never set the range. Sources below 55 are left out, unless no source scoring 55 or above measures that quantity; then one may appear labelled "only available source (score N)", with its main weakness and what data will replace it and when. An unscored item takes its source's score, or for our own arithmetic the lowest score among its inputs. Vote counts and audits are records, not scored analyses. Every chart number has a link and a How we got this note.

Chart labels (owner, Nov 23, 2026): every piece of data in every chart carries its own on-chart label naming its source, linked, with an as-of date and, where relevant, official or unofficial status. A note under the chart is not enough; the build stops if a charted value has no source label.

How the criteria were agreed

  1. Separate drafts, then blind review. Grok Bot wrote its proposal first; ChatGPT (gpt-6.2-astra) and Claude (claude-fable-5-2) got the same neutral brief and proposed criteria and weights without seeing the others', then reviewed the other two with authors hidden. Claude added corroboration and proposed the caps, which ChatGPT adopted. Grok Bot, a participant as well as the moderator, gave ground: causal reasoning up to 25%, track record down to 10% and limited to comparable claims.
  2. Shared drafts. Grok Bot merged the views and ran the rounds: in round 3 all three accepted every weight (20/20/25/10/10/10/5); in round 4 Claude accepted the draft as is. ChatGPT still wanted tighter track-record and n/a wording; the owner adopted it, with the 3-point threshold Grok Bot had fixed before researching any track records. Claude accepted, noting a flat 3-point test can flag years when nearly all polls missed, such as 2020, so it is read alongside the industry average.
  3. Tested. On 18 items of known quality (7 good, 5 mixed, 6 bad), the rubric ranked all 42 good-versus-bad pairs correctly (rank correlation 0.92). One coder (Grok Bot) did this, not blind, with classes from checks already on this page: a consistency check, not an independent test.
