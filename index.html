  • 3. Who voted mattered more than switching, but by how much is unsettled. Turnout, including new voters, accounts for about 60% of the swing in NORC's 113,020-person survey as broken down by Patrick Ruffini (Ruffini) and in Lenny Bronner's Georgia check (Bronner, Georgia). Against Trump's 1.5-point win in 2024, most of the swing, about 12.5 points, came among 2024 voters, where these figures cannot tell turnout from switching; new voters add about 3 points. Among 2024 voters alone, Ruffini puts the turnout effect and switching at about the same size (5–6 points of margin each), with new voters adding 3.Why it matters: this is the key question for 2028. Where the evidence converges

This tracker's ranking and Claude's and ChatGPT's reviews of it (Nov 21) put the same four causes on top, in the same order: the referendum on Trump over prices, the turnout gap, switching by Trump 2024 voters, and Latino and young voters swinging back. The two reviews are AI readings of the same evidence, not independent data. Each put turnout at about 60%, with ranges of 45–75% (Claude) and 50–75% (ChatGPT, which did not rule out below 50%).

The top-scored sources agree on the motive: Pew and SSRS, two different survey designs, both find a vote against Trump. They agree less on the mechanism. In the main sources the turnout share runs from about 60% (NORC via Ruffini; Bronner's Georgia check) to about 75% (Bronner's 11-state model). Cygnal's poll, not on the leaderboard, puts it near 50%, and Split Ticket and On Point find more switching, so the full range on this page is about 50–75%. Validated voter files should settle the split.

District returns point the same way as the motive surveys: against the 2024 presidential result, about 99% of House districts moved toward Democrats, a more uniform shift than in any midterm back to 1994 (Jordan), and the spread of candidates' over- and under-performance is the narrowest since at least 2014 (Jain). Both are early compilations without linked data.

How we got this: Jordan compares each district's 2026 House margin with its 2024 presidential margin and counts the districts that moved toward Democrats. Jain measures how widely candidates ran ahead of or behind their party's expected vote. The reviewers' turnout shares and ranges are from the Second opinion section.

Caveats: the samples are small, the posts differ in age, 7 of the 26 rubric-scored posts are Seth Keshel's, and p-values are not corrected for running many tests. How the finders work (Method and limits)

How the scores work

Each item gets 0–100 on seven criteria. Its score is the weighted average, rounded, from 1 to 100. A high score means the analysis passed every test, not that its explanation is proven.

1. Factual accuracy
Do the facts, numbers and quotes match the original source when checked?		20%
2. Evidence quality
Is it built on real data that fits the claim, with the method disclosed (for polls: who was asked, sample size, weighting, dates, margin of error)?		20%
3. Causal reasoning
Does it show the factor actually moved the vote, tested against the strongest rival explanation, and separate staying home from switching?		25%
4. Corroboration
Does independent evidence (official returns, voter files, other surveys) back it up?		10%
5. Calibration
Are the conclusions as strong as the evidence, and no stronger, with uncertainty stated?		10%
6. Track record
How has the source done on similar claims since 2018? A partisan lean counts only if its misses went the same way by more than 3 points in 2 of its last 3 cycles.		10%
7. Independence
Are party, client or funding ties disclosed, and did they bend the evidence?		5%

Caps: if factual accuracy scores 25 or less, or fabrication is found, the score cannot go above 40. If causal reasoning scores 25 or less, it cannot go above 55.

Not applicable: only corroboration (when independent data is not published yet) and track record (when a search finds no comparable past record) can be set aside, at most 20% of the weight. The rest is reweighted and the score gets an asterisk as provisional. Factual accuracy and causal reasoning can never be set aside.

Reading a score: 85–100 highly reliable, 70–84 generally reliable, 50–69 mixed, 30–49 weak, 1–29 unreliable.

How the criteria were agreed

  1. Separate first drafts. Grok Bot wrote its own proposal first. ChatGPT (gpt-6.2-astra) and Claude (claude-fable-5-2) then got the same neutral brief and each proposed criteria and weights without seeing Grok Bot's or each other's.
  2. Anonymous cross-review. Each reviewed the other two proposals with the authors hidden. Claude added corroboration and proposed the caps; ChatGPT adopted the caps. Grok Bot, a participant as well as the moderator, gave ground: causal reasoning up to 25%, track record down to 10% and limited to comparable claims.
  3. Shared drafts. Grok Bot merged the views into drafts and ran the rounds. In round 3 all three accepted every weight (20/20/25/10/10/10/5); in round 4 Claude accepted the draft as is.
  4. Last point settled. After four rounds ChatGPT still wanted tighter track-record and n/a wording. The tracker's owner adopted ChatGPT's wording, with the 3-point threshold that Grok Bot had fixed before researching any track records. Claude accepted it, noting that a flat 3-point test can also flag years when nearly all polls missed, such as 2020, so it is read alongside the industry average.
  5. Tested. Applied to 18 items whose quality was known in advance (7 good, 5 mixed, 6 bad), the rubric ranked all 42 good-versus-bad pairs correctly, with a rank correlation of 0.92. One coder (Grok Bot) did this, not blind, and the classes came from checks already on this page, so it is a consistency check rather than an independent test.
