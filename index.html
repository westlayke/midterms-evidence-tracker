Summary

Short version, as of Nov 22, 2026 (turnout vs. switching corrected Nov 22). Republicans lost a referendum on President Trump, driven by prices, the economy and tariffs. Most of the roughly 15.5-point swing toward Democrats came from who voted, not from voters changing sides, but the size of each part is not settled. In the main sources, turnout including new voters accounts for about 60% to 75% of the swing: about 60% in NORC's survey as broken down by Patrick Ruffini (Ruffini), 67% in J.L. Partners' Election Day poll (J.L. Partners) and 75% in Lenny Bronner's 11-state precinct model (Bronner). Switching accounts for about 20% to 40% in those sources; Bronner's Georgia check puts it at about 40% (Bronner, Georgia). Two outliers widen the range. Cygnal's poll, not on the leaderboard, splits it about half and half (Cygnal). The exit poll's own crossover rates imply only about 13–22% switching on our arithmetic (below).How we got this: the swing runs from Trump +1.5 in 2024 (49.8% to 48.3%; FEC) to D+13.9 in the unofficial 2026 House count of 118.4 million votes (Popular Vote Analytics; Wikipedia's tally gives D+14.1). That is about 15.5 points; earlier counts were lower (D+12.6 on Nov 6, per On Point). Turnout share = turnout points ÷ total. Ruffini: (5.5 + 3 new voters) ÷ 14 ≈ 60% (57–64% across his 5–6 point ranges). J.L. Partners: 10 of a 15-point swing = 67%. Bronner publishes 75% turnout and 20% switching, leaving 5% unassigned."7% of Trump voters switched" is a share of voters, not of the swing. Our rough arithmetic: 2024 voters were about 85% of 2026 voters (NORC via Ruffini). Trump voters were 46–50% of them: 46% if 35% of Trump voters and 22% of Harris voters stayed home, as J.L. Partners found, or 50% if both stayed home at the same rate. So 6–7% of Trump voters is 2.3–3% of all voters, worth 4.7–6 points of margin, because each switch moves the margin by two votes. Netting out the 3% of Harris voters who voted Republican (NBC) leaves about 2–3.4 points, 13–22% of a 15.5-point swing. Ruffini finds more switching (5–6 points) partly because he corrects recalled 2024 votes for recall bias, which moves points from turnout to switching. His "switching" is net across all 2024 voters, and his "turnout" includes Harris voters turning out more, not only Trump voters staying home.Still open: all of these are surveys or models, and NORC's turnout is modelled, not checked against records. State voter files (expected around the week of Nov 30) should settle the split.
  • 1. It was a vote against Trump. Voters said they were voting against Trump: 43% to 16% in the SSRS Voter Poll (NBC) and 48% to 15% in Pew's post-election survey (Pew), a wider gap than after the 2018 midterm (39% to 25%).Why it matters: this is the motive behind the loss. The election was mainly a verdict on Trump, not on Democrats or on local races, and the anti-Trump vote was bigger than the anti-Biden vote after 2022 (35% to 17%).How sure we are: high. Two large surveys with different designs give the same answer, though SSRS did the fieldwork for both (Pew methods), so they are not fully independent. Open: people report their own motives, and these figures do not show whether the motive worked by changing votes or by keeping voters home.How we got this: each figure is the share of voters who said their House vote was to oppose Trump, or to support him; the rest said he was not a factor. SSRS polled more than 79,000 voters (NBC write-up) and Pew 7,692 voters, Nov 9–15. The 2018 (39–25) and 2022 (35–17, about Biden) figures are the same Pew question in its earlier post-election surveys (Pew topline).
  • 2. Prices, the economy and tariffs drove it. In the SSRS Voter Poll of more than 79,000 voters, 75% rated the economy poorly, 65% said tariffs hurt it and 62% blamed higher prices mostly on Trump's policies (SSRS via NBC).Why it matters: it explains why voters turned on Trump. Other polls agree: J.L. Partners found cost of living and the economy were the top two reasons in every group, On Point found 77.8% of Trump voters who switched to Democrats said gas prices pushed them, from about 65 respondents, which On Point calls directional (On Point), and Navigator's post-election poll (Democratic-aligned, opt-in) found 60% put inflation and the cost of living among their top five issues (Navigator).Still open: in the same poll 63% called the Iran war the wrong decision, and the war pushed fuel prices up at the same time, so surveys cannot cleanly separate the war from prices.How we got this: every figure is a published share of respondents. The SSRS figures (75%, 65%, 62%, 63%) are of all voters. On Point's 77.8% is of the Trump-to-Democrat switchers in its poll. Navigator's 60% is of all its 3,000 registered voters, who ranked up to five issues (23% put inflation first). In J.L. Partners' poll, 33% of 2026 voters named cost of living or the economy as their reason and no other issue topped 9% (J.L. Partners).
  • 3. Who voted mattered more than switching, but by how much is unsettled. Turnout, including new voters, accounts for about 60% of the swing in NORC's 113,020-person survey as broken down by Patrick Ruffini (Ruffini) and in Lenny Bronner's Georgia check (Bronner, Georgia). It is 67% in J.L. Partners' Election Day poll (J.L. Partners) and about 75% in Bronner's 11-state precinct model (Bronner). Among 2024 voters alone, Ruffini puts the turnout effect and switching at about the same size (5–6 points of margin each), with new voters adding 3.Why it matters: this is the key question for 2028. If most of the loss came from Republicans staying home, the party can recover by bringing them back; if it came from switching, the problem is persuasion.How sure we are: fairly sure turnout was the larger part, because a large survey, precinct returns and Georgia's records of who voted all point that way. The exact split is not settled. The sources differ mainly on how big the turnout gap was, NORC identifies who stayed home with a model rather than turnout records, and state voter files (expected around the week of Nov 30) should pin it down.How we got this: turnout share = turnout points ÷ total swing. Ruffini: (5.5 + 3) ÷ 14 ≈ 60%. J.L. Partners: 10 of a 15-point swing (R+2 to D+13) is 67%. Bronner publishes shares directly: 75% turnout and 20% switching in 11 states (5% unassigned), about 40% switching in Georgia. Bronner also says the exit poll implies 35% switching, but our arithmetic from the exit poll's published crossover rates gives 13–22% (see the summary note), so that reading is not used as an estimate here.
  • 4. Switching was real but modest. About 7% of Trump 2024 voters who voted backed a House Democrat (CNN), and about 1 in 10 backed the Democratic Senate candidates who won in Texas, Ohio, Iowa, Kansas and South Carolina (same CNN report). NORC finds the same 7% (Robinson, NORC data). NBC's write-up of the poll gives 6%, and 3% of Harris voters went the other way (NBC). These are shares of Trump voters, not of the swing; see the note above for how they relate.Why it matters: switching was not the main story, but about 7 in 100 Trump voters crossing over is enough to flip close races, and it was larger in the red states where Democrats won Senate seats.How sure we are: fairly sure of the 7%, because SSRS and NORC, two separate surveys, both find it. Other polls find more (J.L. Partners about 12% of those who voted, On Point 15.1%), partly because they count on different bases, so the true size is still debated.How we got this: 7% is CNN's share of self-reported Trump 2024 voters who voted in 2026 and backed the House Democrat; 'about 1 in 10' is CNN's figure for those five Senate races, not House races. NORC's 7% is the share of Trump voters who voted and backed the House Democrat in Robinson's chart. J.L. Partners says 'just under one in ten Trump voters (8%) switched' without stating the base. If the base is all 2024 Trump voters, 35% of whom did not vote, that is 8 ÷ 0.65 ≈ 12% of those who voted; this is our assumption, not J.L.'s figure (J.L. Partners). On Point's 15.1% counts registered Trump voters who voted for, or would have voted for, a Democrat, so it includes nonvoters' stated preferences (On Point).
  • 5. Latino and young voters swung back. In majority-Hispanic House districts the Democratic margin rose about 22 points from Harris's 2024 result, against about 14 in districts under 10% Hispanic (Morris; district returns, 2026 House vs. 2024 president). Youth turnout was about 27%, CIRCLE's early estimate (range 25–29%). That matches its early estimate for 2022 and trails only 2018 in 30 years, so young voters swung mainly in how they voted, not in how many turned out (CIRCLE).Why it matters: these groups had moved toward Trump in 2024, so their return undid much of his coalition gain. SSRS (via NBC, Nov 4) has Latino voters at 62% for House Democrats after 46% for Trump in 2024, and men under 25 at 68–28 Democratic after 49–48 for Trump (NBC). CNN's later tables of the same poll have Latinos at 65–33 (per Morris).How sure we are: high on direction, because election returns back the surveys. Less on size: the 2024 figures come from different surveys, so each swing mixes methods.How we got this: Morris's 22 and 14 points are the change in the Democratic margin from Harris's 2024 result to the 2026 House vote, for districts grouped by Hispanic share. 27% is CIRCLE's exit-poll-based estimate of turnout among young voters, with a 25–29% range. The 62% and 68–28 are SSRS figures for 2026 House votes; the 46% and 49–48 are the 2024 figures as NBC reports them.
  • 6. Texas is the exception. There, Bronner's model finds voters switching to Democrats explained more of the Democratic gain than turnout did, the only state where it did (Bronner, Texas, Profoundd). That fits voters punishing the ticket for Ken Paxton. Ray Tye's county analysis finds both at work: about 27% of 2024 Trump voters stayed home against about 14% of Harris voters, while 2–8% of Trump voters backed Talarico (Ray Tye).Why it matters: it shows candidates still mattered in places. Only 34% of Texas voters rated Paxton's character positively (SSRS Texas sample; NBC).What it does not show: weak candidates did not drive the national result. Candidates' over- and under-performance had its narrowest spread since at least 2014 (Jain), so candidates explain a few states, not the wave.How we got this: Bronner's Texas result is his precinct model run on 12 counties holding 63% of the vote (Bronner, Texas). 34% is the share of SSRS's Texas voters who rated Paxton's character positively. Tye's 27% and 14% are his two-method estimates of 2024 voters who skipped 2026, and 2–8% his range for Trump voters who backed Talarico. Jain's spread is the range of candidates' results against their party's expected vote, from his chart (no linked data).
  • 7. Redistricting limited the losses rather than causing them. Against the old maps, Republicans still gained about 4–7 seats, the New York Times' preliminary estimate from comparing the results with the maps they replaced (NYT, via PollTracker).Why it matters: this rules out a popular explanation, that the mid-decade map changes backfired. Headlines saying so count outcomes (CNN: Republicans flipped just 2 of the 15 districts they targeted, with a Louisiana runoff pending; CNN), while the New York Times compared the result with the old maps, which answers the question of cause. North Carolina may yet count as a slight backfire: Cain and Schneer say it will if the Democrat wins NC-3, which was still being counted (Cain and Schneer).How sure we are: fairly sure of the direction; the 4–7 seat range is a model estimate.How we got this: the New York Times estimated how many seats the 2026 vote would have given each party on the maps in place before 2025 and compared that with the actual result; 4–7 is its preliminary range. CNN's 2 of 15 counts the targeted districts Republicans flipped, using CNN's race calls as of its Nov 17 update.
  • 8. The Trump voters who stayed home look recoverable. They still lean Republican for the House (73% to 10%) but are more moderate and sourer on Trump (Robinson, NORC data).Why it matters: most of the Trump voters who skipped 2026 would still have voted Republican, so the loss does not look like a permanent shift. But compared with Trump voters who turned out they are more moderate (44% vs. 27%), gloomier on the economy (61% vs. 39% say it is poor) and more likely to disapprove of Trump (45% vs. 20%), so they are not safe Republican votes.Still open: who stayed home comes from NORC's model, not turnout records, and Morris reports that his November poll finds many of them now prefer Democrats (Morris; behind a paywall, not checked here).How we got this: every figure is from Robinson's chart of NORC's 2024 Trump voters (Nov. 6 weights), comparing 1,760 whom NORC's likely-voter model counts as staying home with 34,353 who voted. Each pair is the share of stay-homes vs. voters giving that answer. The House preference leaves out the 19% of stay-homes who gave none.

Where the evidence converges

This tracker's ranking and Claude's and ChatGPT's reviews of it (Nov 21) put the same four causes on top, in the same order: the referendum on Trump over prices, the turnout gap, switching by Trump 2024 voters, and Latino and young voters swinging back. The two reviews are AI readings of the same evidence, not independent data. Each put turnout at about 60%, with ranges of 45–75% (Claude) and 50–75% (ChatGPT, which did not rule out below 50%).

The top-scored sources agree on the motive: Pew and SSRS, two different survey designs, both find a vote against Trump. They agree less on the mechanism. In the main sources the turnout share runs from about 60% (NORC via Ruffini; Bronner's Georgia check) to about 75% (Bronner's 11-state model). Cygnal's poll, not on the leaderboard, puts it near 50%, and Split Ticket and On Point find more switching, so the full range on this page is about 50–75%. Validated voter files should settle the split.

District returns point the same way as the motive surveys: against the 2024 presidential result, about 99% of House districts moved toward Democrats, a more uniform shift than in any midterm back to 1994 (Jordan), and the spread of candidates' over- and under-performance is the narrowest since at least 2014 (Jain). Both are early compilations without linked data.

How we got this: Jordan compares each district's 2026 House margin with its 2024 presidential margin and counts the districts that moved toward Democrats. Jain measures how widely candidates ran ahead of or behind their party's expected vote. The reviewers' turnout shares and ranges are from the Second opinion section.

How the finders work (Method and limits)

How the scores work

Each item gets 0–100 on seven criteria. Its score is the weighted average, rounded, from 1 to 100. A high score means the analysis passed every test, not that its explanation is proven.

1. Factual accuracy
Do the facts, numbers and quotes match the original source when checked?		20%
2. Evidence quality
Is it built on real data that fits the claim, with the method disclosed (for polls: who was asked, sample size, weighting, dates, margin of error)?		20%
3. Causal reasoning
Does it show the factor actually moved the vote, tested against the strongest rival explanation, and separate staying home from switching?		25%
4. Corroboration
Does independent evidence (official returns, voter files, other surveys) back it up?		10%
5. Calibration
Are the conclusions as strong as the evidence, and no stronger, with uncertainty stated?		10%
6. Track record
How has the source done on similar claims since 2018? A partisan lean counts only if its misses went the same way by more than 3 points in 2 of its last 3 cycles.		10%
7. Independence
Are party, client or funding ties disclosed, and did they bend the evidence?		5%

Caps: if factual accuracy scores 25 or less, or fabrication is found, the score cannot go above 40. If causal reasoning scores 25 or less, it cannot go above 55.

Not applicable: only corroboration (when independent data is not published yet) and track record (when a search finds no comparable past record) can be set aside, at most 20% of the weight. The rest is reweighted and the score gets an asterisk as provisional. Factual accuracy and causal reasoning can never be set aside.

Reading a score: 85–100 highly reliable, 70–84 generally reliable, 50–69 mixed, 30–49 weak, 1–29 unreliable.

How the criteria were agreed

  1. Separate first drafts. Grok Bot wrote its own proposal first. ChatGPT (gpt-6.2-astra) and Claude (claude-fable-5-2) then got the same neutral brief and each proposed criteria and weights without seeing Grok Bot's or each other's.
  2. Anonymous cross-review. Each reviewed the other two proposals with the authors hidden. Claude added corroboration and proposed the caps; ChatGPT adopted the caps. Grok Bot, a participant as well as the moderator, gave ground: causal reasoning up to 25%, track record down to 10% and limited to comparable claims.
  3. Shared drafts. Grok Bot merged the views into drafts and ran the rounds. In round 3 all three accepted every weight (20/20/25/10/10/10/5); in round 4 Claude accepted the draft as is.
  4. Last point settled. After four rounds ChatGPT still wanted tighter track-record and n/a wording. The tracker's owner adopted ChatGPT's wording, with the 3-point threshold that Grok Bot had fixed before researching any track records. Claude accepted it, noting that a flat 3-point test can also flag years when nearly all polls missed, such as 2020, so it is read alongside the industry average.
  5. Tested. Applied to 18 items whose quality was known in advance (7 good, 5 mixed, 6 bad), the rubric ranked all 42 good-versus-bad pairs correctly, with a rank correlation of 0.92. One coder (Grok Bot) did this, not blind, and the classes came from checks already on this page, so it is a consistency check rather than an independent test.
