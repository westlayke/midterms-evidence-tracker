Summary

Short version, as of Nov 22, 2026. Republicans lost a referendum on President Trump, driven by prices, the economy and tariffs. About 60% of the swing came from who voted (Trump 2024 voters staying home, Democrats and new voters turning out) and about 40% from Trump voters switching sides. Across methods, the turnout share runs from 50% to 75%.
  • Voters said they were voting against Trump: 43% to 16% in the SSRS Voter Poll (NBC) and 48% to 15% in Pew's post-election survey (Pew), wider than after 2018 (39% to 25%).
  • Prices drove it: 75% rated the economy poorly, 65% said tariffs hurt it and 62% blamed higher prices mostly on Trump's policies (SSRS via NBC).
  • Turnout did more than switching: NORC's 113,020-voter survey, as broken down by Patrick Ruffini, puts it at about 60% turnout and 40% switching (Ruffini); Lenny Bronner's precinct model puts turnout at 75% (Bronner).
  • Switching was real but modest: about 7% of Trump 2024 voters who voted backed a House Democrat, about 1 in 10 in Texas, Ohio, Iowa, Kansas and South Carolina (CNN).
  • Latino and young voters swung back: majority-Hispanic districts moved about 22 points toward Democrats, against about 14 in districts under 10% Hispanic (Morris); youth turnout of 27% was the second highest for a midterm in 30 years (CIRCLE).
  • Texas is the exception: there persuasion, not turnout, explains most of the Democratic gain, which fits voters punishing the ticket for Ken Paxton (Bronner, Profoundd).
  • Redistricting limited the losses rather than causing them: against the old maps, Republicans still gained about 4–7 seats (NYT via PollTracker).
  • The Trump voters who stayed home still lean Republican (73% to 10% for the House) but are more moderate and sourer on Trump, so they look recoverable (Robinson, NORC data).

Where the evidence converges

This tracker's ranking and independent reviews by Claude and ChatGPT put the same four causes on top, in the same order: the referendum on Trump over prices, the turnout gap, switching by Trump 2024 voters, and Latino and young voters swinging back. All three put turnout at about 60% of the swing.

The top-scored sources agree on the motive: Pew and SSRS, two different survey designs, both find a vote against Trump. They agree less on the mechanism. Estimates of the turnout share run from 50% (Cygnal) to 75% (Bronner), and Split Ticket and On Point find more switching. Validated voter files should settle the split.

Does engagement track quality?

No. Among the 12 analyst posts scored blind on a 0–12 scale, more views went with lower quality (Spearman −0.68, p = 0.015). In the widened sample of 62 posts the link disappears (+0.22, p = 0.08). On the agreed 1–100 rubric, the 23 scored X posts show no link with views (−0.14, p = 0.51), and higher-scored posts drew fewer likes, reposts, replies, quotes and bookmarks per view (−0.55, p = 0.007).

Caveats: the samples are small, the posts differ in age, 7 of the 23 rubric-scored posts are Seth Keshel's, and p-values are not corrected for running many tests. Metrics fetched Nov 21, 2026, 9:00 AM ET.
