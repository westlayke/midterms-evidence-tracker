Prompt used by Grok to guide this research

The research instructions given for this report, gathered into one brief. Copy it to rerun or extend the work.

GOAL
Find out why Republicans lost the November 2026 midterm elections so badly. Favor thoroughness over speed.

PROCESS
1. Baseline facts: establish the actual results (House popular vote, seats, Senate, governors, turnout) from official or near-official counts, so every claim can be checked against them.
2. Search X broadly, in rounds: named analysts and forecasters, turnout vs. vote switching, exit and voter polls, demographic groups, issues (economy, prices, tariffs, the Iran war), candidate quality, redistricting, polling error. Widen each round from what the last one found.
3. Keep only posts that bring statistical analysis or data: a named source, a dataset, or a stated method. Drop bare assertions, unsourced statistics and chatbot summaries.
4. Verify: open the underlying source behind every kept post and check its numbers against it. Drop posts whose numbers cannot be traced.
5. Score track records: for each forecaster, pollster or analyst, assess forecasting accuracy in the 2018 through 2026 elections, including calibration (for example Brier scores) and any consistent partisan lean in their misses, using third-party scorecards where possible.
6. Flag bias: note each source's partisan affiliation, funding, clients and incentives.
7. Cluster and weigh: group the evidence by cause and weigh each cause by how many independent methods (returns, precinct data, voter files, surveys) agree, noting where they conflict.
8. Rank: label each cause strongly supported, contested, or weakly supported / rejected, with a confidence level, and list the open questions that better data could settle.

EXIT POLLS AND SURVEYS
Include an exit poll or survey only if it is transparent about its method, how respondents were selected, sample size, weighting and mode. Otherwise exclude it and say why.

REPORT FORMAT
- Publish as a web page that is updated over time.
- Open with an executive summary of dated entries, newest first. Each entry names the report or data currently judged most accurate, says what new information changed the thinking, and, when a new study takes the lead, explains why it is superior to the prior leader. Link each entry to the detailed sections.

ENGAGEMENT ANALYSIS
- Show engagement metrics for every cited X post (views, reposts, likes, replies, quotes, bookmarks), with the date they were fetched.
- Near the top of the report, analyze how engagement correlates with the quality of the analysis, using the quality factors above (method, verification, data type, track record, bias, strength of the claim).
- Score quality blind: strip metrics, follower counts and earlier scores from the posts, shuffle them, and score against a rubric written in advance; join scores to metrics only after scoring. Make the track-record factor discriminate, or drop it and say so.
- Widen the sample with widely seen X posts from Nov 3-20, 2026 that make causal claims about the Republican loss without data, plus mid- and low-engagement posts, chosen by an inclusion rule fixed in advance and reported. Exclude Grok replies. Use these posts only in the engagement analysis, never as evidence for a cause.
- Report correlations for analysts only, data-backed posts only and the full widened sample, with robustness checks, and compare with the earlier non-blind result.

ONGOING UPDATES
Check for new evidence daily at about 7 AM Mountain Time. Add a new executive-summary entry only when something meaningful changes.

INTEGRITY
Never fabricate data. Every claim needs a real source; mark anything unknown as unknown.

Executive summary

Short version, as of Nov 21, 2026. The premise holds: this was the largest Republican defeat in the national House vote since 1974. The best current evidence puts most of the swing on who voted (Trump 2024 voters staying home, Harris voters turning out, and new voters): about 60% to 75%, with switching at about 20% to 40%. NORC as broken down by Ruffini is the switching-heavy end (about 60/40), and on it the turnout effect among 2024 voters and switching were about the same size. (Range restated Nov 22, 2026; see Update 12.) Those who voted said they were voting against President Trump over prices, tariffs and the Iran war; why the stay-homes stayed home is less certain. (Corrected Nov 21, 2026 after the second-opinion review; the earlier wording said staying home did more damage than switching, which the leading source does not show.)

Entries are newest first. Each one names the source currently judged most accurate, what shaped that view, and, when a new study takes the lead, why it was judged better. The links under each entry jump to the evidence.

Turnout vs. vote switching

Share of the Democratic gain each analysis attributes to turnout (including new voters). The rest is switching.

Agreement: most post-election methods put turnout-related effects at 60–75% of the swing. A Cygnal post-election poll (about 50%) and Rational Ground's survey compilation (about 64%) widen the range to 50–75%; see Deep dive: web analyses of turnout vs. switching. Ruffini's county measure suggests about 70% of Trump 2024 voters voted versus about 90% of Harris voters, and the turnout gap was worst for Republicans in Mississippi (−34), Maine and Iowa (−30), and best in Florida (−6) and Nebraska (+4), the states where Republicans held on.

Conflict: Split Ticket's Lakshya Jain says his registered-voter polling showed an abnormally high number of Trump voters flipping, and that many who stayed home would have voted Democratic. On Point Polling found 15.1% of Trump voters backed a House Democrat; J.L. Partners found 8%. Texas is the clear exception to the turnout story: Bronner's model has persuasion explaining more of the Democratic gain there than turnout, which fits Ruffini's read that voters punished the whole ticket for Ken Paxton.

Note on the switching figures: they use different bases. Bronner's 4 of every 100 Trump 2024 voters is 6% of the Trump voters who voted. J.L. Partners' 8% of all Trump voters is about 12% of those who voted. On Point's 15.1% is as reported. The Quantus Insights thread uses the lowest of these numbers; see Deep dive: Quantus Insights thread.

Why it matters: if most of the swing was turnout, Republicans can recover in 2028 by bringing their voters back. Morris argues against that reading: his November poll finds many of the Trump 2024 voters who stayed home now prefer Democrats. A new cut of the NORC data (below) points the other way.

Who stayed home (added Nov 21, 2026): Jonathan Robinson, formerly Catalist's research director, split NORC's 2024 Trump voters by whether they voted (34,353 voted, 1,760 stayed home; NORC's Nov. 6 weights; 2024 vote self-reported; staying home is NORC's likely-voter model, not validated turnout). The stay-homes backed the Republican for the House 73% to 10% (19% gave no preference and are excluded), against 91% to 7% for those who voted. They were more moderate (44% vs. 27%), gloomier on the economy (61% vs. 39% say it is poor), more likely to disapprove of Trump (45% vs. 20%), less likely to call themselves MAGA (41% vs. 66%), and 55% had skipped 2022 (vs. 14%). They skew younger and less white, but per Robinson the approval, ideology and economy gaps hold after matching on age and race, while the MAGA gap shrinks by half. Reading: most would have voted Republican, so they are recoverable, but they are softer than the party's base and sour on Trump.

Checked: the figures that overlap with NORC numbers reported elsewhere match: about 1,750 stay-homes, 91% to 7% among Trump voters who voted, 58% of stay-homes calling themselves Republicans, and Trump approval of 54% to 44% among stay-homes and 80% to 20% among voters (Rational Ground's compilation). The House-preference, ideology and MAGA figures could not be checked against NORC directly.

Add-back estimate (added Nov 21, 2026): Will Jordan (Democratic pollster, Global Strategy Group) put the roughly 50 million 2024 voters who skipped 2026 back in with their NORC preferences: they voted about 60–40 Trump in 2024 and lean about 50–38 in 2026, giving a national margin of about D+6 instead of D+14. His post labels the 50–38 as D-R, but D+6 only follows if it favors Republicans: about 119 million House votes at D+14 plus 50 million at R+12 gives about D+6. On that reading, 2024 voters staying home account for about 8 of the roughly 15.5-point swing from Trump's 2024 margin, a bit over half, before counting new voters. That is inside this report's 50–75% range, a little above Ruffini's 40% for 2024 voters' turnout alone. It is a back-of-the-envelope figure with no published method.

  • No independent data: every number traces to a source this report already weighs, so the thread adds no evidence for or against turnout. Its weight comes from Bronner and J.L. Partners, not from Quantus.
  • Ecological inference: the 63/4/33 split is a model estimate of individual behavior from precinct totals. It is not observed. If Trump voters stayed home in the same precincts where new Democratic voters turned out, or where switching happened, the model has to assume them apart. Bronner said on Nov 6 that a fuller method was still to come and that his estimates did not yet include voter-file data. Quantus presents the split as fact, with no range.
  • Coverage: the precinct data cover 11 to 13 states, some only partly (12 Texas counties, 24 in Michigan). The thread applies the numbers to every 100 Trump voters nationally.
  • Selective pairing: the stay-home rate comes from J.L. Partners and the switching rate from Bronner, who has the lower switching number. Each source's own split (75/20 and 67/33) gives switching a larger role than the thread suggests.
  • Survey limits: J.L. Partners is an opt-in quota sample relying on recalled 2024 votes, with a ±10.5-point margin on defectors, weighted to an assumed turnout scenario.
  • Incentive: Quantus's own final polls missed Texas, Georgia and North Carolina by 10.7 to 13.1 points toward Republicans. A surge in turnout that the polls could not see is also how Corley explained his firm's miss, so the firm has a stake in this account.
  • What would settle it: state voter files (expected around the week of Nov 30) will show who actually voted, which pins down the turnout side. Measuring switching directly still needs validated-voter studies (Catalist, Pew), usually months later.

Bottom line: the thread's direction matches the 60–75% turnout range in this report, and its numbers fall at the turnout-heavy end, close to Bronner's. Its claim that very few switched is not supported, even by its own sources. Switching was a real and sizable minority of the swing.

What the web consensus says

  • The switching rate is the most agreed number: about 7% of Trump 2024 voters who voted backed a House Democrat (SSRS, NORC, YouGov), about 1 in 10 in the red-state Senate races (SSRS), 11% in Texas. As a share of all Trump 2024 voters, including those who stayed home, that is roughly 5%, because a quarter to a third of them did not vote. The outliers, On Point (15%) and J.L. Partners (8% of all, about 12% of those who voted), are small opt-in samples.
  • Turnout share: data-backed post-election pieces put turnout, including new voters, at 50% (Cygnal) to 75% (Bronner); the median of the four with numbers is about 65%. This report's leader, Ruffini on NORC, says 60%, and it is the only one that adjusts for recall bias.
  • Why the estimates differ: they agree on the switching rate and disagree on the size of the turnout gap. Surveys put the gap between Trump and Harris voters' turnout at 4 to 11 points; Ruffini's county measures suggest 10 to 20. Surveys of voters only (SSRS, Cygnal) have to infer the gap from recalled 2024 vote, which can make staying home look bigger than it was.
  • The persuasion-first view (Cohn, Wasserman) rests mostly on Texas, where three independent measures agree switching dominated: Bronner's precincts (45% switching), the ABC/SSRS Texas poll (an electorate Trump would have carried by 7 went to Talarico by 12) and Cohn's early-voter estimates (Trump +6 to Talarico +12). Outside Texas, neither has published evidence.
  • Denominators: “6% of Republicans” (party ID), “7% of Trump voters who voted”, “4 of every 100 Trump voters” and “20% of the gain” are four different measures. Brookings reaches a turnout-only story partly by using party ID, and the New York Post by crediting all turnout effects to Trump voters staying home.
  • Against this report's leaders: no web piece has better data than NORC (113,020 registered voters, voters and nonvoters). The highest-scoring piece, CNN's, reports the SSRS Voter Poll, already the runner-up. The leader is unchanged.
  • What will settle it: state voter files (expected about the week of Nov 30) will show who actually voted, which pins down the turnout gap; Morris has promised numbers from them. Measuring switching directly still needs validated-voter studies (Catalist, Pew), usually months later.
